Inside the hollow backglass of a 1954 Wurlitzer jukebox, folded flat against the neon tube housing, there was a document my stepdaughter had spent six months trying to make disappear. She thought the machine was worthless junk, a dead hulk she could haul to a scrap yard. She mailed me a four thousand one hundred dollar token check to close out my half of a business worth seven hundred thirty thousand dollars, and she had a notary stamp from a dead woman to back it up. By the end of this, she was polishing pinball playfields in a bright orange vest that said Tilted My Trust, and every high score card in my arcade printed her fraud docket number under the player's name. The shape of the ending is this: she lost everything, I kept the arcade, and the machine she called scrap became the vault that saved me.

I was fifty seven years old, thirty one years as a school custodian for the Garfield Independent School District, and I knew exactly how to strip a pinball playfield down to bare wood and repaint a flipper wear mark until you couldn't find it with a jeweler's loupe. In March of 2022 I cashed out one hundred twenty four thousand dollars of my pension to start an arcade with my stepdaughter Marlene. She was the business side, the one with the spreadsheet smile and the notary app on her phone. I was the hands. The partnership agreement gave me fifty percent, notarized at a table in the back office of the First Midwest Credit Union, and it included a lifetime of free game nights for my grandchildren, no blackout dates, no coin drop, ever. My husband Gordon, Marlene's father, had died eleven months after the arcade opened. The only thing he left me besides the house and a garage full of tools was a piece of advice I should have listened to sooner. His daughter loved things more than people.

The first machine we restored together was a 1976 Bally Captain Fantastic with a decaying backglass and a dead solenoid driver board. I rebuilt the flipper coils by hand, resoldered the cold joints on the power supply, and replaced the score display with a Nixie tube set from a defunct jukebox. Marlene set up the route contracts with three bars on the east side, a laundromat in Mesquite, and a pizza parlor near the community college. The revenue split was sixty five thirty five, coin drop and bill validator, with a service call clause that Marlene had drafted to charge fifteen dollars extra if the machine ate a quarter. That machine printed the first free game night receipt for my grandson Danny on a Friday in June, a thin thermal slip with his score on the Captain Fantastic and the words FREE PLAY printed across the bottom. By the second year we had eleven machines on route, four restored jukeboxes, and a service van with a rack of spare solenoids, rubber rings, and a tube tester. The gross revenue was just under twelve thousand a month before coin splits. Golden Token Amusement Group offered seven hundred thirty thousand for the restored machines and the route contracts, based on a multiple of route EBITDA, three point two times trailing twelve months, including a noncompete clause for the Dallas metroplex. Marlene called it our exit. I called it our retirement.

The discovery started with a flat white envelope. It came certified mail on a Tuesday, return receipt requested, signature required. The letter claimed the partnership had been dissolved by mutual consent on March 2, that all assets of Ballyhoo Vintage Arcade LLC had been sold to Golden Token Amusement Group for seven hundred thirty thousand, and that after settling outstanding route lease obligations and the promissory note on the Captain Fantastic pinball machine, my remaining equity share after depreciation and legal fees came to four thousand one hundred dollars. A token check. The letter said my signature had been witnessed by a notary named Sandra Obeck, a name I had never seen, on a document I had never signed. Sandra Obeck, I later learned, had died of a stroke in 2019 in Reno, Nevada, and her notary commission had expired before she stopped breathing.

Within a day Marlene and Brett arrived at my front door. They didn't knock. Brett used the garage code I had never given him but Marlene had probably watched me type on the keypad when she came to borrow my leaf blower. The accident happened on a Monday morning.
