My name is Dana Holloway. For twenty four years, three months, and eleven days, I was the daughter who stayed. My father, Arthur Holloway, was a master carpenter with a union card and a set of chisels he kept sharp enough to shave with. He built that lakefront cottage by himself over three summers, using cedar shingles, hand-cut oak beams, and salvaged stone from an old barn foundation. When his body started failing him at sixty-one, I was twenty-nine. I stayed. I managed the medication schedules, the insurance appeals, the late night hospital runs, the physical therapy visits, the wound care, and the endless phone calls with Medicare. I learned how to shim a door, sweat a copper pipe, and stretch a fixed income so far it practically snapped. My half-sister Renee was seven years older and had a different mother. She left for a corporate marketing job in Phoenix the year our dad first got sick. She visited twice in two decades, once for a wedding and once for a funeral. Her husband Marcus was the kind of man who wore cufflinks to a barbecue and used the word synergy in casual conversation. They were the ones who would sweep in at the end, because that is what vultures do. They circle. They wait. They manage.

My father passed away on a Tuesday morning in early spring. He died in his own bed, in the lakefront cottage he built with his own hands, looking out at the water he loved. It was peaceful. I was holding his hand. He squeezed my fingers once, looked at the door, and said, "Keep the hinges oiled." Then he was gone. I didn't understand the meaning of that sentence for another ten days.

The cottage itself was worth about four hundred and fifty thousand dollars, because of the lakefront access. The land was the value. The house was modest, three bedrooms, a stone fireplace, a dock that jutted out into the cove. The will was straightforward, and I heard it read aloud four days after the funeral, in a small conference room at a law office that smelled like old books and coffee. The estate attorney, a man named Gerald who had known my father for thirty years, cleared his throat and read the relevant clause. I received a life estate in the cottage, meaning I had the legal right to live there, use it, enjoy it, and maintain it for the rest of my natural life. Upon my death, the remainder interest would pass to Renee. It was a classic estate planning structure, designed to protect a caregiver child from being displaced by a sibling who had not been present. I was never going to own it outright. I was never going to be rich. But I was never going to be homeless. That was the plan. Renee got the rest of the financial accounts, the tiny IRA, the old truck. The life estate was my shield, and I was supposed to be safe inside it.

Renee and Marcus did not come to the will reading. They sent a faxed letter from Marcus's office requesting a copy of the will and all pertinent filings. They came inside, sat at the kitchen table, and Marcus slid a file folder across the wood. "We need to get a handle on the estate," he said. "The property needs to be liquidated.
